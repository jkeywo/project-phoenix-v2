Thin Margin is optimistic operational science fiction about capable people keeping fragile systems, relationships, and communities working under pressure. It takes place in the same broad setting as *The Neutral Zone*, but earlier: before the Alliance’s war with the power later called the Dynasty, while that power is still known as the Imperium. Phoenix does not need to use the Neutral Zone region, its treaty map, or its post-war political conditions. Its broad tonal reference is the *Star Trek* era of *The Next Generation*, *Deep Space Nine*, and *Voyager*: an ensemble of professionals uses knowledge, technology, diplomacy, courage, and, when necessary, force to solve difficult problems.

## Canon relationship

The durable fictional layer inherited from *The Neutral Zone* includes the Alliance, the polity later called the Dynasty, its Houses and Honour culture, the hidden Mandate, and the Singularity. Phoenix adapts that layer to an earlier era and a potentially different region of space. The later game’s Neutral Zone Treaty, thirty-year post-war premise, named frontier systems, Nanophage outbreak, rogue warship, interregnum politics, and faction positions created by the war do not automatically apply.

This distinction follows the source setting’s WorldSpec contract: setting truth can be shared while each game owns its mechanics, scenario pressures, and local cast. When the later material describes a House after the war, Phoenix may inherit the House’s enduring identity but must not assume that later territorial loss, grievance, leadership, or alliance has already happened.

### Working chronology

| Era | Relationship to Phoenix |
|---|---|
| Imperium era | Phoenix’s present. The Alliance and Imperium coexist before the war; rivalry, contact, covert pressure, local conflict, and House ambition are possible without treating the future war as inevitable. |
| Alliance–Imperium war | Future history relative to Phoenix. Its causes, exact date, course, and Phoenix campaign relationship remain open. |
| Dynasty / Neutral Zone era | The period used by *The Neutral Zone*, thirty years after the war. The former Imperium is then called the Dynasty, and the same Houses compete within changed political conditions. |

Phoenix episodes should not foreshadow the future through characters who somehow know the canon. The setting can contain pressures that later contribute to war, but player success may still create meaningful peace, rescue, justice, and local change. Shared history is a frame, not a command that every scenario must end in failure.

## The meaning of the Thin Margin

The setting’s “thin margin” is the small reserve between an operating society and cascading failure: the spare reactor capacity, remaining shield arc, safe traffic window, disputed berth, repair team, reliable scan, political goodwill, or promise that lets people recover rather than collapse. Technology is powerful, but systems are connected and capacity is finite. Institutions can do great things when their people coordinate; they can also become rigid, self-protective, or unjust without becoming cartoonishly evil.

The “thin margin” formulation is a Phoenix synthesis rather than inherited Neutral Zone canon. It gives current material—Combat Test’s defended station, Falling Skyway’s failing lift and allocation crisis, ship power reserves, directional defence, and distributed bridge authority—a shared dramatic language without fixing unexplored history prematurely.

## Tone pillars

### Competence creates drama

Characters generally understand their jobs and act on intelligible interests. Drama comes from incomplete knowledge, finite time, conflicting duties, physical limits, institutional incentives, and consequences—not from everyone ignoring an obvious solution. A mistake may still happen, but it should reveal pressure or character rather than serve as the only way the plot can begin.

### Hope is work

The setting is hopeful because cooperation, evidence, restraint, repair, and courageous decisions can improve an imperfect situation. Hope does not guarantee a clean outcome. Some missions should end with damage, compromise, or people left behind, while still showing that professional action mattered.

### Force is an instrument

Combat is legitimate and may be the whole point of a direct scenario. In broader stories, force sits beside negotiation, rescue, navigation, science, logistics, and deterrence. Violence has tactical, political, and human consequences. Enemies should remain actors with objectives and capabilities, not an undifferentiated species of targets.

### State tells the truth

If dialogue says a tether is failing, the infrastructure condition should be failing. If a captain promises passage, the commitment should constrain later choices. If a storm blocks sensors, both human and AI observation should suffer. Fiction earns trust when narrative, interface, and simulation are different views of the same event.

### The bridge is an ensemble

No officer is merely there to confirm the protagonist’s decisions. Each role has expertise and authority worth hearing. Dialogue and scenario structure should create moments when the best Captain action is to ask, the best specialist action is to recommend, and the whole crew has to choose between valid priorities.

## Scale and everyday reality

Thin Margin focuses on inhabited systems, stations, orbital infrastructure, shipping lanes, patrol areas, and the organisations that depend on them. It does not need a continuously simulated galaxy, terrain-level planetary flight or a continuous civilian-life model. A future coarse Orbital Mode may turn a planet into a wrapping operational map of points of interest while keeping the playable situation bounded and bridge-scale.

Infrastructure matters culturally as well as mechanically. A skyhook, depot, convoy, starbase, or comms relay exists because people rely on it. Their failures create work, bargaining power, migration, danger, and moral pressure. Civilian traffic should make space feel inhabited without requiring every ship to become a full character.

## Institutions and factions

### The Alliance

The Alliance is an exploratory and humanitarian interstellar polity whose ship crews exercise substantial local discretion while remaining answerable to public legitimacy. Its professional starship tradition combines technical, diplomatic, scientific, medical, logistical, and protective responsibilities rather than separating exploration from duty of care. Alliance crews should act with confidence without being infallible, and institutional disagreement should arise from competing responsibilities rather than a simple enlightened-versus-corrupt split.

The current internal `Federation` faction identifier is a technical legacy, not a second polity. Player-facing fiction should use **Alliance** unless a future migration deliberately changes the runtime identifier. The Alliance’s constitution, territorial scale, central institutions, rank system, and exact pre-war relationship with the Imperium remain open.

### The Imperium and the later Dynasty

In the Phoenix era, the other great power is the **Imperium**. In the later Neutral Zone era it is known as the **Dynasty**. It is organised through competing Houses whose authority combines military power, territory, obligation, patronage, and claims to legitimacy. The Houses are not a single hostile species or uniform ideology; they can cooperate, compete, trade, negotiate, feud, protect dependants, pursue reform, or use deniable force.

**Honour** is the Imperium’s political and cultural account of obligation, grievance, status, and recognised conduct. Honour can be spent, owed, lost, contested, and restored. It should create legible commitments and leverage rather than function as an exotic synonym for aggression. Different Houses and individuals interpret honour differently.

The later setting’s principal Houses already exist in Phoenix’s era:

| House | Durable identity available to Phoenix | Earlier-era caution |
|---|---|---|
| Harrow | Expansionist, direct and martial; associates strength, victory and recognised glory with rightful authority | Do not import its later post-war territorial gains or succession position without authoring them as future developments |
| Requiem | Cultured and reform-facing; seeks legitimacy through enlightened appearance, control of timing, and institutional change | Its later losses and weakened standing have not yet occurred |
| Graven | Weapon-smiths, fortification builders and strategic industrialists who value durability, production and hard assets | Later fortress projects and treaty obligations are not automatically present |
| Cinder | A House suited to grievance, improvisation, mobility and promises of return | Its later dispossession is an outcome of history still ahead and cannot be assumed in Phoenix |
| Vyre | Secretive, technically adept and disciplined; turns information asymmetry, deniable action and private power into survival | Accusations should remain evidence-led, not proof that every Vyre actor is treacherous |

Minor Houses recovered from the later game include Dusk and Ven, with Ashkar, Talon, Mire and Quill currently less-settled placeholders in that source. They are available for future Phoenix development but should not receive high-level canon by inference alone. The shared branch language—Blades combining Security/Operations, Void combining Tactical/Science, and Hearth combining Engineering/Medical—may inform Imperial ship and crew organisation once Phoenix decides whether it already exists in this era.

### House Harrow

The current “Harrow” ship family is best understood as House Harrow military content within the Imperium, not a separate species or polity. Its disciplined roles from pickets to capital artillery fit a House that values decisive strength and visible command. Combat Test may use those ships as a clean opposing force, while story scenarios should identify the particular command, objective, rules of engagement, and relationship to wider Imperial authority.

House Harrow actors need not be inevitable enemies. They may be rivals, patrol authorities, allies of necessity, protectors, claimants, or an internal Imperial faction whose methods place them in conflict with the crew. Their directness should contrast with Alliance procedure without reducing either side to virtue or villainy.

### The Mandate

The Mandate is a hidden destabilising organisation whose embedded agents seek to sharpen Alliance–Imperium tension while keeping their own existence and authority deniable. Later Neutral Zone material associates it with covert warrants, the Throne, investigation and paranoia, but its exact origin and command relationship remain provisional setting truth. Phoenix can use Mandate pressure without revealing a complete conspiracy or making every disagreement its work.

Mandate plots should exploit real grievances, ambiguous evidence, institutional incentives, covert logistics, and choices players could plausibly make without manipulation. Discovery should come from authoritative evidence and provenance. “The Mandate did it” must not erase the responsibility or agency of visible actors.

### The Singularity

The Singularity is a distant existential and environmental pressure associated in the later era with ancient Nodes, wormhole routes, spores, synthetic or gene-altering pathogens, and the Nanophage. It is not merely another navy with different ship silhouettes. Its presence should challenge scientific understanding, containment, ethics, logistics, and cooperation as well as defence.

Phoenix occurs earlier, so authors must decide what is known in this period. A Singularity episode may involve an artefact, dormant system, anomalous ecology, signal, or limited incursion without importing the later Nanophage outbreak wholesale. Origin claims should remain uncertain until players establish them through evidence.

### Havelock and organised interests

Falling Skyway presents Havelock operations as a corporate or institutional claimant with records, workers, traffic interests, and armed enforcement. Havelock is Phoenix-local rather than inherited Neutral Zone canon unless later setting work connects it. It should be written as an organisation with incentives and internal people, not a shorthand for “the bad corporation.” Evidence may expose misconduct and power may be used coercively, while individual representatives still speak from plausible duties and fears.

### Workforces, civilians, and local authority

Workers are not a faction enemy. A strike is an operational and political state, not hostility. Civilian crews, committees, station control, convoy leaders, riggers, and local officials should possess specific needs and limited agency. Their disposition toward the crew may differ from military faction relations.

## Technology principles

- Technology should be comprehensible at the decision level even when its physics is fictional. Players need to know what a shield focus, scan band, repair team, impulse charge, transfer capacity, or comms range means operationally.
- Technobabble may colour dialogue but must not conceal the actionable fact.
- Automation is ordinary and bounded. AI can operate systems, rank candidates, and maintain a ship, while responsibility and value judgements remain meaningful human work.
- Sensors reveal reality with limits. They do not create scenario facts merely because the script wants the player to know them.
- Ships and infrastructure are repairable working systems, not magic until the plot declares them broken.
- Physical scale is authored for readable bridge play. Exact astrophysical scale yields where necessary to coherent tactical and operational distance.

## Dialogue and prose

Voice should be concise, professional, and specific under pressure. Characters may disagree strongly without all becoming sarcastic or contemptuous. Humour can emerge from personality, professional familiarity, or the absurdity of difficult work, but should not puncture consequences at the moment they matter.

Briefings should state the situation and immediate responsibility without explaining every hidden truth. Operational updates should name what changed, why it matters, and who is speaking. Choices should describe the commitment or action, not a moral alignment label. Important responses deserve confirmation when they bind the crew.

Avoid universal quips, constant military barking, faux-archaic space jargon, or decorative strings of invented nouns. Prefer “Depot B is below certified transfer load” over “the quantum transfer manifold is destabilising” unless the manifold is a real system the crew can inspect or repair.

## Naming principles

Names should make the world navigable before they make it exotic. Organisations, ship classes, proper names, locations, and operational structures need different patterns. Player-facing ship names such as AEV Phoenix can carry an institutional prefix; hostile or local names may use different conventions once ratified. Machine identifiers and localisation keys remain separate from displayed names.

Current unratified bracketed names in `strings.csv` are placeholders, not setting law. A later naming guide should define Alliance vessel prefixes, Harrow self-designation versus Alliance reporting names, Havelock brands/divisions, geographic conventions, and personal-name cultures.

## Visual presentation

The bridge interface should look like professional equipment designed to support decisions, not a puzzle made of decorative panels. Geometry, hierarchy, text, icons, and motion carry meaning before colour. Dense technical surfaces may exist, but the urgent state and available action remain clear on a phone and the shared viewscreen remains readable across a room.

Alliance presentation should suggest integrated, dependable systems and a shared institutional design language. Imperial presentation may vary by House while retaining enough common material culture to read as one political sphere; Harrow silhouettes and effects should communicate tactical role before exact class recognition. Civilian and infrastructure assets should show function and scale. Damage, shield impacts, weapon arcs, engine state, storm bands, and operations should make authoritative state visible without turning the screen into continuous noise.

The viewscreen supplies shared drama: scale, approach, damage, environmental movement, and the consequence of crew decisions. Private consoles supply exact role-specific state. Cinematic camera use may heighten a transition or outcome, but must not obscure a time-critical decision.

## Audio presentation

Audio should make the ship feel like one place: restrained ambient machinery, distinct alert posture, system-specific cues, impacts that communicate direction or severity where possible, and scenario music that supports rather than dictates emotion. Critical cues require visual/text equivalents. Repeated warnings need priority and cooldown so a damaged ship does not become unintelligible.

Dialogue is currently text-led. If voiced material is introduced, the complete transcript, speaker identity, urgency, and response choices must remain available. Venue effects such as lighting and smoke may deepen presentation through optional adapters but can never be the sole carrier of state.

## Content boundaries

- The default tone is not grimdark, nihilistic, or casually cruel.
- Horror, atrocity, prejudice, labour conflict, political coercion, and civilian harm may appear only with scenario-specific purpose, suitable presentation controls, and consequences; they are not ambient texture.
- The game does not require graphic bodily injury. Ship, structure, and operational consequence can carry stakes.
- Conflict should not depend on every culture being monolithic.
- The setting should not imply that automation makes people disposable or that technical competence automatically resolves political legitimacy.
- An episode may be simple and clean. Complexity is available, not compulsory.

## Scenario review questions

1. Are the actors competent, and does each have a reason for its behaviour?
2. Which thin margin is under pressure, and can the crew observe it through authoritative state?
3. Does the scenario allow the relevant mix of technical work, communication, restraint, or force without pretending every option is equally wise?
4. Is the immediate action legible beneath the fiction?
5. Do consequences acknowledge promises, evidence, damage, casualties, and institutional interests where relevant?
6. Does every station with relevant expertise have a reason to contribute?
7. Does the ending preserve hope as earned agency rather than guaranteed comfort?

## Open setting decisions

- Exact date and political conditions of Phoenix relative to the future Alliance–Imperium war.
- Phoenix’s region, local powers, political map, travel model, and relationship to the later Neutral Zone frontier.
- The formal nature of the Alliance and Imperium, their current diplomacy, and whether the future war has identifiable early causes.
- Runtime migration from the internal `Federation` identifier to the player-facing Alliance name.
- Which Imperial branch traditions already exist, how Houses share authority, and the current status of minor Houses.
- House Harrow’s Phoenix-era leadership, internal factions, language, self-understanding, and relationship to local operations.
- Havelock’s legal status, scale, and relationship to both major factions.
- The Mandate’s actual authority and how much of it players can learn in this era.
- What the Alliance and Imperium know about the Singularity before the later Nanophage crisis.
- Rank vocabulary, uniforms, personal naming, calendars, currencies, and civilian governance.
- Limits and social consequences of AI, cloning, medicine, communication, and faster-than-light travel.
- Final visual, musical, and voice references that are original to Thin Margin rather than expressed through *Star Trek* comparison.
